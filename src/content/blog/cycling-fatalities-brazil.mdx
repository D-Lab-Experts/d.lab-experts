  <h2 id="contexto">1. Why this study exists</h2>

  <p>Cycling in Brazil <span class="hl-white">kills about four people a day, and the number isn't falling</span>. Despite this, the country has an information problem: no official database classifies the crash by the <strong>purpose of the ride</strong>. The data does not tell us who crashed on the way to work and who crashed while training. Health systems record the mechanism of the crash, not the purpose of the person cycling.</p>

  <p>IMPS lives with this gap in practice: it maintains a collaborative mapping of incidents precisely because the official record is incomplete. This study formalizes, with method, what can be asserted from what already exists, and <span class="hl-lilac">makes explicit what still cannot be measured</span>.</p>

  <p>The D.Lab reading treats road safety as <span class="term-def" data-def="An analytical framework in which each agent (pedestrian, cyclist, driver, public manager) chooses a route, time, equipment, or policy from a perception of risk that may or may not be calibrated to the real risk. When perception drifts from the data, the decision gets worse.">decision-making under uncertainty</span>: a person chooses route, time, and equipment from a perception of risk that may or may not be calibrated to the real risk. Calibrating that perception, with auditable public data, is part of what makes an applied study deliver policy value.</p>

  <div class="biz-insight">
    <div class="biz-insight-eyebrow">Why you should read this</div>
    <div class="biz-insight-title">If you work in mobility, public health, road safety, or urban policy, this study delivers three things that matter:</div>
    <ul>
      <li>An auditable count of deaths and hospital admissions, with three independent methods that converge.</li>
      <li>The separation between urban and sport cyclists, with the uncertainty range made explicit and the correct algebra for combining sources.</li>
      <li>A reading of where policy effort should go, anchored in mechanism, victim profile, and geography.</li>

  <h2 id="metodo">2. Question, method, and source hierarchy</h2>

  <p><strong>Central question.</strong> What can public data reliably assert about cyclist crash incidence in Brazil, its severity, and the split between urban and sport use?</p>

  <h3>Objectives</h3>
  <ol style="padding-left:22px;color:var(--muted);line-height:1.85;margin-bottom:24px">
    <li>Estimate cyclist mortality and morbidity and validate those counts through independent methods.</li>
    <li>Describe the severity of events across three levels: minor, serious, and fatal.</li>
    <li>Characterize who dies and who gets hurt (age, sex, geography, mechanism).</li>
    <li>Estimate the proportion between urban and sport cyclists.</li>
    <li>Document the confidence level of each finding and the remaining gaps.</li>
  </ol>

  <h3>How we classified the sources</h3>
  <p>A <span class="term-def" data-def="Research that gathers and re-analyzes data already produced by third parties (official agencies, scientific literature, industry), without primary collection. It requires an explicit method for selecting and reconciling sources.">secondary-data</span> study, with no primary collection and no submission to a journal, published open access. Every figure was classified by the reliability level of its source, and the conclusions rest only on the most robust sources.</p>

    <div class="data-card-title">Source hierarchy adopted</div>

  <h3>The set of methods used</h3>
  <p>The reconciliation combined direct counting of microdata, application of WHO proportions, set algebra, and a <span class="term-def" data-def="A procedure to check the study's internal consistency by recomputing each key figure through two or more paths and verifying that the orders of magnitude match. It works as a cross-check on the cited sources.">consistency audit</span> with seven independent checks. Each is described in the reproducibility section.</p>

  <div style="display:flex;flex-wrap:wrap;gap:8px;margin:14px 0 22px">
    <span class="method-badge term-def" data-def="A direct count of the microdata from the Mortality Information System (SIM), filtering victims coded as cyclists in the ICD-10 underlying cause.">SIM count</span>
    <span class="method-badge term-def" data-def="Applies the WHO proportion of cyclists in total road deaths to the total number of deaths in the reference year, producing an estimate independent of the microdata.">WHO method</span>
    <span class="method-badge term-def" data-def="A count of hospital admissions in SIH, with the caveat that SIH only captures those who reach the public health system (SUS). It does not add to SIM; they measure different things.">SIH count</span>
    <span class="method-badge term-def" data-def="Set algebra to estimate overlapping populations: Total = A + B − Both, rather than A = Total − B.">Set algebra</span>
    <span class="method-badge term-def" data-def="Triangulation across independent estimates to identify the consensus range and discard outliers.">Triangulation</span>
    <span class="method-badge term-def" data-def="A list of cross-checks that recomputes each finding by more than one route and verifies that the orders of magnitude match one another.">Consistency audit</span>
  </div>

  <h2 id="mortalidade">3. Mortality: stable at a high level</h2>

  <p>SIM records close to 1,300 cyclist deaths a year. The series shows a trough in 2016 (1,262) and a rebound to 1,381 in 2021. Between 2014 and 2024, there were <span class="hl-white">14,834 deaths</span>. In historical perspective, cyclists' share of road deaths rose from 1% (396 deaths) in 1998 to 4% (1,556) in 2008, and stabilized at the current level.</p>

  <h2 id="validacao">4. Cross-validation by three methods</h2>

  <p>The count does not come from a single source. Three independent methods converge on a narrow range:</p>

  <ul style="color:var(--muted);line-height:1.85;padding-left:0;list-style:none;margin-bottom:24px">
    <li style="padding-left:22px;position:relative;margin-bottom:10px"><span style="position:absolute;left:0;color:var(--lilac);font-weight:700">→</span> <strong>Direct SIM:</strong> ~1,300 deaths/year, underlying-cause count.</li>
    <li style="padding-left:22px;position:relative;margin-bottom:10px"><span style="position:absolute;left:0;color:var(--blue);font-weight:700">→</span> <strong>WHO method:</strong> 3.4% of road deaths × 38,651 deaths in 2016 = 1,314.</li>

  <h2 id="perfil">6. Who dies and who gets hurt</h2>

Geographically, about 60% of deaths occur in the South and Southeast, with São Paulo leading in hospital admissions.</p>

  <h2 id="implicacoes">11. Implications for public policy and mobility</h2>

  <blockquote><p>Campaigns and infrastructure designed for the sport cyclist do not capture the public that dies most. Policy should target utility commuting on shared roads.</p></blockquote>

  <div class="biz-insight">
    <div class="biz-insight-eyebrow">Recommendation 1 · For municipal managers</div>
    <div class="biz-insight-title">Focus on the shared road, not the weekend bike path</div>
    <p>60% of deaths are vehicle-strike collisions with cars. The marginal safety gain from segregation on the main road is greater than that of new bike paths in parks. Prioritize <span class="hl-white">bike lanes in commuting corridors</span>, speed reduction on shared roads, and treatment of intersections with a crash history.</p>
  </div>

  <div class="biz-insight">
    <div class="biz-insight-eyebrow">Recommendation 2 · For the Ministry of Health and IBGE</div>
    <div class="biz-insight-title">Measuring the cyclist by purpose is a prerequisite</div>
    <p>As long as the official database does not classify the crash by the purpose of the ride, any national policy operates on incomplete data. Adding a purpose variable to PNAD, PNS, or the VIVA form itself would be <span class="hl-white">the only cheap, structural intervention</span> that unlocks the remaining uncertainty in this study.</p>
  </div>

  <div class="biz-insight">
    <div class="biz-insight-eyebrow">Recommendation 3 · For media and public communication</div>
    <div class="biz-insight-title">The athlete narrative hides the real victim</div>
    <p>Media coverage that frames cycling as a sport tends to reinforce the imagery of a middle-class hobby. The typical victim is <span class="hl-white">the lower-income urban worker</span> going to or from work. Recalibrating the framing is part of the public health effort.</p>
  </div>

  <div class="biz-insight">
    <div class="biz-insight-eyebrow">Recommendation 4 · For advocacy organizations</div>
    <div class="biz-insight-title">Collaborative mapping is a legitimate complementary source</div>
    <p>The direct cost to the public health system (SUS) for injured cyclists, about R$ 15 million a year, is a fraction of the <span class="term-def" data-def="The total cost of road crashes estimated by IPEA, which includes lost productivity, property damage, pre-hospital care, and insurance. The cyclist's share is not broken out, but the ~R$ 15 M from SUS is a proven floor, not a ceiling.">true social cost</span>, since it does not include deaths or lost productivity. The total cost of road crashes is estimated by IPEA at R$ 28 billion a year. While the official record is incomplete, initiatives such as the IMPS collaborative mapping are a legitimate complementary source.</p>
  </div>
